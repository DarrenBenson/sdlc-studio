# RFC-0057: A queue of planned sprints, so the planner and the runner can be different people

> **Status:** Draft
> **Size:** L
> **Affects:** .claude/skills/sdlc-studio/scripts/sprint.py, .claude/skills/sdlc-studio/reference-sprint.md, .claude/skills/sdlc-studio/help/sprint.md
> **Date:** 2026-07-27
> **Created-by:** sdlc-studio file
> **Raised-by:** Claude Fable 5 (operator-raised, RUN-01KYHVWK resume discussion); agent; skill v5.0.0

## Summary

Today a sprint is planned and run by the same actor in the same breath: `sprint plan --write` opens the one run slot immediately, and the only multi-sprint facility is the rolling policy (`--cycles N`), which fixes a standing POLICY once and regenerates the PLAN at each boundary. The operator asks for something the rolling policy cannot give: 'plan the next 5 sprints' as inspectable, orderable, editable things, then later 'run the next sprint' - possibly by a different person, or the same person on a different day. The ask carries a CRUD surface with it: show the next sprint with its goal and contents, insert one, cancel the next, clear the planned set, reorder by WSJF.

The tension is recorded and must be answered rather than stepped around. sprint.py's rolling-policy design note states plainly that queueing plans up front does not work, because the backlog is GENERATED by the work - a cycle raises bugs and lessons - so a frozen queue rots while it waits. That argument is sound and this RFC does not dispute it. What it observes is that the argument defeats queueing a frozen BATCH, and the operator's need is not for a frozen batch: it is for the intent to exist as an artefact before the work starts, so a second person can see it, judge it and run it. Separating the planner from the runner is the same move the project already makes for author and reviewer, and it is not available anywhere in the sprint surface today.

A hard constraint falls out of that. Both `run-state.json` and `goal-review.json` live under a gitignored `.local/`, so neither travels with the repo (see CR0440). A queue that a second person is meant to run cannot live there: whatever is queued has to be committed, or the handover it exists to enable cannot happen.

## Design Options

- **O1 Queue frozen plans. Each queued sprint holds its resolved batch of unit ids, fixed at planning time. This is the literal reading of the request and the one the recorded design note already refutes: by the time sprint four runs, the bugs sprints one to three raised are absent from it and units it names may be delivered, deferred or superseded. Cheap to build, rots exactly as documented.**
- **O2 Queue charters, materialise the batch at start. A queued sprint records INTENT - the Sprint Goal, its recorded seat review, a scope rule (an epic, a status query, a worklist, a WSJF rank) and an appetite - but not a resolved id list. `sprint next` reads the head of the queue and plans it against the backlog AS IT IS AT THAT MOMENT, which is precisely the regeneration the rolling policy already does, just triggered by a queued charter rather than by a standing policy. The queue is committed, so a second person can read, reorder and run it. Preserves the rot argument in full while delivering separation, inspectability and CRUD.**
- **O3 Extend the rolling policy with per-cycle goals. Keep `--cycles N` and let each cycle carry its own goal and scope. Smallest change, but the plans still never exist as artefacts before they run, so nothing can be shown, reordered or cancelled, and the runner must be the actor who set the policy - the separation is not delivered.**
- **O4 Decline. The rolling policy is the answer to unattended multi-sprint execution, and the planner/runner split is served by an operator telling an agent which units to plan. Costs nothing, leaves the handover ad hoc and leaves 'show me the next sprint' unanswerable.**

## Recommendation

O2. It is the only option that separates planning intent from batch resolution, which is what makes the recorded rot objection survivable: nothing is frozen except the goal and the scope rule, and those are exactly the parts a human judgement was applied to and should not be silently redone. It also makes the existing seat review of a goal portable - today a reviewed goal sits in gitignored local state and is re-reviewed per goal string, whereas a committed charter carries its review with it to whoever runs it.

**The rot objection assumes a static current sprint, and the operator's reframing removes that assumption.** A frozen queue rots only if the sprint in flight cannot absorb what the work generates. If the running sprint can be corrected - bring a unit in and take one of similar size out, add a whole epic and raise the appetite to match, call the sprint at a point and descope what was never started - then a queued charter never has to be right at planning time; it has to be correctable at run time. That is a materially weaker requirement, and it is the one the recorded design note never considered, because those controls do not exist to consider.

What exists today is the thinnest possible version: `sprint batch add <id>` and `batch drop <id> --reason` mutate one unit at a time and are entirely capacity-blind - no points, no appetite, no report that a 5 was swapped for a 2 - there is no bulk add by epic, and the appetite is fixed at plan time with no verb to change it. `sprint stop` abandons a run rather than calling it at a point and descoping the remainder. So the batch is mutable in name and static in practice, which is why the rot argument reads stronger than it is. The in-flight controls are therefore not a nice-to-have alongside the queue - they are the precondition that makes queueing defensible at all, and they carry their own value immediately, since an open run today cannot be resized or rebalanced honestly either. The capacity-aware subset is settled enough to build now and is filed as CR0441; the close-semantics half stays here as D5.

Open decisions for the operator before decomposition: (D1) where a charter lives - a new `sdlc-studio/sprints/` artefact type with ids and an index, matching every other artefact, versus a single committed queue file that is cheaper but has no per-sprint history; (D2) what `sprint next` does when a charter's scope resolves to nothing at start time - skip to the next charter, stop and report, or refuse - the rot argument resurfacing at materialisation rather than being avoided by it; (D3) whether running a charter requires its goal review to have been recorded by someone other than the person running it, which would make planner/runner separation a gate rather than a convention, consistent with the two-role review rule; (D4) whether WSJF ordering is recomputed at each `next` or fixed when the queue is authored; (D5) what "call the sprint here" means at the close - whether descoping the unstarted remainder is an honest close recording what was achieved against the goal, or a stop, and what happens to the descoped units (returned to the backlog, carried to the next charter, or left where they are). This is close semantics rather than batch mechanics, which is why it stays an open decision while the capacity-aware controls proceed as CR0441.
